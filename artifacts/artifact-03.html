Artifact Content

Albert Einstein said, “If you can’t explain something simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” With that mindset, I wanted to explain neural networks in a way that anyone, even someone with no technology background, can follow. To do this, I applied the communication for learning strategies from this week’s lesson.

A neural network is like a team of tiny helpers working together to solve a big problem. Each helper (a “neuron”) makes one small decision. A single helper does not accomplish much, but when thousands are organized in layers, they become powerful pattern finders. This layered structure is similar to how our own brain processes information. The first layer notices basic lines or colors, deeper layers combine those into shapes, and final layers recognize full objects through practice.

Here is a simple real-world example. Imagine training a system to recognize handwritten digits, like reading a 5 or 9 from a form.

  • Input layer: The network takes in raw pixel values of the image. It does not understand anything yet; it simply receives information.
  • Hidden layers: Each neuron looks for small features such as curves, corners, or strokes. As data moves through more layers, these tiny features come together into meaningful shapes.
  • Learning step: When the network guesses incorrectly, it compares its guess to the correct answer. It then adjusts the strength of its internal connections, called weights, similar to how we adjust the pressure of our hand when correcting a writing mistake. This process repeats thousands of times, gradually improving accuracy.
  • Output layer: The final layer gives a prediction like “This is a 5,” “This email looks like spam,” or “This transaction seems suspicious.”

This gradual improvement enables neural networks to learn patterns without requiring step-by-step rules.

The effectiveness of this explanation rests on the communication strategies used, which are listed below:

  • Chunking information into simple steps to prevent overload.
  • Storytelling and analogy, using tiny helpers and handwriting to make neural networks easy to visualize.
  • Progressive disclosure, starting with familiar concepts before introducing technical ideas like weights.
  • Relevance and real-world examples, linking neural networks to everyday uses such as digit recognition and spam detection.
  • Visual thinking, painting a mental picture of layers, curves, pixels, and patterns.

These communication strategies are essential for change leadership. When organizations adopt AI or ML, employees may feel intimidated or overwhelmed. Leaders who explain technology through relatable metaphors build trust, reduce resistance, and create psychological safety. Clear communication turns a complex concept into something approachable, which is exactly what successful change leaders must do when guiding teams through digital transformation.
