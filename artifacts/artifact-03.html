Artifact 3 — Explaining Neural Networks Simply

Course: AIML-500 · Machine Learning Training Methods & Communication

Introduction: This artifact explains neural networks in plain, story-driven language for a non-technical audience. It also demonstrates how I applied communication-for-learning strategies—chunking, storytelling, progressive disclosure, and relevance—to support change leadership when introducing AI/ML concepts.

Objective

  • Explain what a neural network is using simple, concrete language.
  • Show how communication strategies make complex AI topics approachable.
  • Connect the explanation to change leadership and AI adoption in organizations.

Class Context

This was created for the 3.4 discussion, Communicating for Learning. The task: explain neural networks so clearly that anyone could understand, while intentionally using this week’s strategies.

Process

  1. Reviewed course material on communication for learning and change leadership.
  2. Chose familiar analogies (tiny helpers, handwritten digits) to avoid jargon.
  3. Structured the explanation as four steps: input layer → hidden layers → learning step → output layer.
  4. Iterated wording for brevity, visual thinking, and progressive disclosure.
  5. Added a reflection linking this style to real organizational adoption of AI.

Plain-Language Explanation

“If you can’t explain something simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” With that mindset, I framed neural networks like a team of tiny helpers. Each helper (a neuron) makes a small decision. One helper can’t do much, but thousands organized in layers become powerful pattern finders—similar to how our brain processes information (lines → shapes → objects).

Handwritten-digit example:

  • Input layer: receives raw pixels—no understanding yet, just information.
  • Hidden layers: detect tiny features (curves, corners, strokes); deeper layers combine them into meaningful shapes.
  • Learning step: when the guess is wrong, the network compares to the correct answer and adjusts internal weights—like adjusting pen pressure to fix handwriting. Repeated thousands of times.
  • Output layer: produces a prediction such as “This is a 5,” “This looks like spam,” or “This transaction seems suspicious.”

This gradual, repeated improvement lets neural networks learn patterns without step-by-step rules.

Communication Strategies Used

  • Chunking content into simple steps to reduce overload.
  • Storytelling & analogy (tiny helpers, handwriting) to aid visualization.
  • Progressive disclosure—start familiar, introduce technical terms later.
  • Relevance—tie to everyday uses (digit recognition, spam detection, fraud alerts).
  • Visual thinking—invite a mental image of layers, curves, pixels, patterns.

Value Proposition

I bridge technical AI/ML concepts and non-technical audiences. For employers, this demonstrates clear explanation, change-management awareness, and communication that reduces resistance while increasing engagement—useful in roles that combine data, communication, and leadership.

Reflection

I learned that explaining AI is as much about empathy as accuracy. Removing jargon, breaking ideas into steps, and connecting to real-world experiences made the concept approachable. Iterating with chunking, storytelling, and progressive disclosure strengthened my confidence to communicate AI clearly to different audiences—critical for successful AI adoption.
