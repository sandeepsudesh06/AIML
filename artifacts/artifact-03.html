Artifact 3 — Explaining Neural Networks Simply

Introduction: This artifact presents my explanation of neural networks written in simple, relatable language so that anyone—even someone with no technical background—can understand it. I used storytelling, analogy, chunking, and progressive disclosure to make the topic clear and approachable.

Explanation

Albert Einstein said, “If you can’t explain something simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” With that mindset, I explained neural networks using familiar ideas and everyday examples.

A neural network is like a team of tiny helpers working together to solve a big problem. Each helper (a “neuron”) makes one small decision. One helper alone does not accomplish much, but when thousands are organized into layers, they become powerful pattern finders. This layered structure is similar to how our own brain processes information. The first layer notices simple things like lines or colors, deeper layers combine those into shapes, and final layers recognize complete objects through repeated practice.

Here is a simple real-world example: training a system to recognize handwritten digits like reading a 5 or 9 from a form.

  • Input layer: The network receives raw pixel values from the image. It does not understand anything yet; it simply takes in information.
  • Hidden layers: Each neuron looks for small features such as curves, corners, or strokes. As data moves through more layers, these tiny features combine into meaningful shapes.
  • Learning step: When the network guesses incorrectly, it compares its guess to the correct answer. It then adjusts the strength of its internal connections (weights), similar to how we adjust the pressure of our hand when correcting a writing mistake. This process repeats thousands of times, gradually improving accuracy.
  • Output layer: The final layer gives a prediction like “This is a 5,” “This email looks like spam,” or “This transaction seems suspicious.”

This gradual improvement enables neural networks to learn patterns without requiring step-by-step rules.

Communication Strategies Used

  • Chunking information into simple steps to prevent overload.
  • Storytelling & analogy using “tiny helpers” and handwriting examples.
  • Progressive disclosure starting with familiar concepts before adding technical ones.
  • Relevance & real-world examples such as digit recognition, spam detection, and fraud alerts.
  • Visual thinking painting a mental picture of layers, curves, pixels, and patterns.

Why This Matters

Clear communication is essential when organizations adopt AI or ML. Employees may feel intimidated or overwhelmed, but relatable explanations build trust and reduce resistance. Turning complex ideas into simple, understandable language helps teams adapt to digital transformation with confidence and curiosity.
